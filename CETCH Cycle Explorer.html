The CETCH cycle

Evolution produced six pathways for turning CO2 into biomass. In 2016 Tobias Erb's group drew a seventh on paper, assembled it from seventeen purified enzymes, and ran it in a test tube. This page takes it apart one reaction at a time.

01 — The design brief

Why build a carbon-fixation pathway from scratch?

Rubisco is the most abundant enzyme on Earth and one of the slowest. It turns over a few times per second, and it cannot reliably tell CO2 from O2: in a C3 leaf in air, roughly one in every three or four reactions is an oxygenation, which produces 2-phosphoglycolate and sets off photorespiration to clean it up.

Decades of attempts to fix Rubisco have moved the needle very little. The alternative is to stop fixing Rubisco and start from the carboxylase you would rather have. Erb's group had one: enoyl-CoA carboxylase/reductases (ECRs), a family of enzymes from bacterial acetyl-CoA assimilation that carboxylate a C=C double bond using NADPH, with no biotin, no ATP, and no measurable reaction with oxygen.

From there the design runs backwards — the approach the authors call metabolic retrosynthesis. Pick the target product. Pick the carboxylase. Then search enzyme databases for a sequence of reactions that returns the carboxylase's product to its own substrate, closing a cycle. The chemistry is constrained by what enzymes exist, not by what evolution happened to assemble in one organism.

The reducing power is identical. Every saving is in ATP, and nearly all of it comes from the carboxylase: Rubisco's substrate has to be regenerated as a bisphosphate, which costs phosphoryl transfers that an acyl-CoA cycle never pays. The single remaining ATP in CETCH activates 4-hydroxybutyrate to its CoA thioester.

Worth arguing about in class

Two steps in CETCH 5.4 — Pco and Mco — pass their electrons straight to O2 and make H2O2, which catalase then destroys. In the natural ethylmalonyl-CoA pathway the equivalent dehydrogenase hands those electrons to an electron-transfer flavoprotein, where the cell can recover them. So per turn the cycle discards two pairs of electrons while paying for four NADPH. The ATP ledger looks excellent; the electron ledger looks wasteful. Whether that trade is worth making depends on what is scarce — and in a test tube fed by formate dehydrogenase, reducing power was cheap and rate was not.

04 — Making it run

Version 1.0 did not work

A pathway that balances on paper is not a pathway that turns in a tube. Seventeen purified enzymes in one pot means seventeen pH optima, seventeen stabilities, and a great many opportunities for one enzyme to act on another's intermediate. The cycle reached its published performance only after several rounds of redesign, and the published version is numbered 5.4 for a reason.

Three kinds of problem had to be solved.

  • One step set the pace. Specific activities in the mix spanned five orders of magnitude — catalase around 11,700 U mg⁻¹ and mesaconyl-CoA hydratase around 1,500 U mg⁻¹ at one end, 4-hydroxybutyryl-CoA synthetase near 3.9 U mg⁻¹ and methylsuccinyl-CoA oxidase near 0.1 U mg⁻¹ at the other. Flux through a cycle is set by its slowest member, and in a closed loop a slow step does not merely limit output: it strands the carbon pool in one intermediate.
  • So the slow enzyme was rebuilt. The natural methylsuccinyl-CoA dehydrogenase needs an electron-transfer partner the test tube does not have. The group re-engineered it into an oxidase that reduces O2 directly, removing the need for any artificial electron mediator. This change alone is reported to have improved the cycle's performance about twenty-fold. Three of the seventeen enzymes in the final set are engineered variants rather than wild-type proteins.
  • Side products were sent back, not designed away. Promiscuous enzymes make dead ends — Ccr, for instance, can reduce its enoyl-CoA substrate without carboxylating it, producing a saturated acyl-CoA that no other enzyme in the cycle will touch. Rather than engineer the promiscuity out, the authors added enzymes that convert the dead end back into a cycle intermediate. They call this metabolic proofreading, and it is the most transferable idea in the paper: in a synthetic pathway you can afford a correcting enzyme more easily than a perfect one.

The catalysts, in order

The seventeen proteins come from nine organisms across bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes — the acyl-CoA oxidase, for example, is ACX4 from Arabidopsis peroxisomes. Alongside the core catalysts the mixture carries catalase and a cofactor-regeneration set that keeps NADPH and ATP supplied.

07 — Afterwards

What the cycle can and cannot yet do

CETCH 5.4 fixes CO2 at about 5 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ of protein, against roughly 1–3 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ for the Calvin cycle's enzymes. That is a genuine result and a modest one: it is an in vitro rate, in a mixture tuned for the purpose, with cofactors regenerated by added enzymes.

Two limitations matter most for anyone thinking about putting it in a cell. The product, glyoxylate, is reactive and poorly connected to central metabolism — it needs a partner such as acetyl-CoA or propionyl-CoA before it becomes anything useful. And the cycle runs on CoA thioesters, a metabolite class a chloroplast does not maintain at the needed concentrations, in a compartment that already contains a complete Calvin cycle competing for the same CO2.

Progress since has gone in three directions. The cycle has been driven by light rather than by added cofactors, inside droplets containing spinach thylakoid membranes — an artificial chloroplast. Its reaction conditions have been optimised by active machine learning over roughly a thousand experimental combinations, improving productivity around ten-fold. And its output has been extended downstream, through the β-hydroxyaspartate cycle and into acetyl-CoA, terpenes and polyketides, which makes the pathway a route to products rather than only to biomass.
